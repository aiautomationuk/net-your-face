Tips for Better Sleep Hygiene UK

27 November 2026

Tips for better sleep hygiene UK readers can rely on start with the NHS basics: keep regular hours, wind down for an hour before bed, and make your bedroom dark, quiet and cool. Sleep hygiene simply means the everyday habits and surroundings that help you sleep well. This guide sets out what the NHS and The Sleep Charity recommend, and when it is time to see your GP.

Quick answer: The main tips for better sleep hygiene in the UK are to keep a regular sleep schedule, relax for an hour before bed, keep your bedroom dark, quiet and cool, avoid caffeine, alcohol and big meals late in the day, and put screens away before bedtime. The NHS says adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep on average. If changing your habits has not helped, see your GP.

Key Takeaways

  • A regular routine and an hour to wind down are the first things the NHS suggests for better sleep.

  • A dark, quiet, cool bedroom with comfortable bedding makes it easier to sleep.

  • Cutting back on caffeine, alcohol and screens in the evening removes common causes of a bad night.

  • See your GP if sleep problems persist after you have changed your habits, or if they affect your daily life.

  • This guide is general information, not medical advice, and it does not cover sleeping tablets or supplements.

Understanding Sleep Hygiene: Why It Matters in the UK

What Is Sleep Hygiene?

Sleep hygiene refers to the habits and practices that promote regular, good-quality sleep. It covers everything from your bedtime routine to the way you set up your bedroom. The NHS says adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep on average, and that insomnia usually gets better by changing your sleeping habits.

The Importance of Sleep Hygiene for Health

A run of bad nights can leave you tired, irritable and struggling to concentrate. NHS Every Mind Matters includes sleep among its mental wellbeing tips, which is why the importance of sleep hygiene goes beyond feeling rested. For more everyday habits that help, see our guide on how to improve mental wellbeing.

Common Sleep Problems in the UK

Insomnia is the most familiar sleep problem: regularly finding it hard to get to sleep, waking several times in the night, or waking early and not getting back to sleep. The NHS lists stress, anxiety or depression, noise, a room that is too hot or cold, an uncomfortable bed, alcohol, caffeine or nicotine, and shift work among the most common causes. Spotting which of these applies to you makes the tips below easier to put into practice.

Where UK Sleep Advice Comes From

The most reliable sleep advice in the UK comes from the NHS, through its insomnia pages and its Every Mind Matters sleep tips. The Sleep Charity also publishes practical guidance on bedrooms and routines. There are no legal rules about how you sleep, so treat these as trusted guidance to follow at home. For more on keeping your bedroom clean and fresh, read our post on washing bedding for a fresh bedroom.

Practical Tips for Better Sleep Hygiene UK

Establish a Regular Sleep Schedule

Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, including weekends, helps your body settle into a rhythm. The NHS advises sticking to your regular hours even after a bad night, instead of sleeping in. It also advises against napping during the day if you are struggling to sleep at night.

Create a Restful Bedroom Environment

Your bedroom should be a calm place kept for sleep. The NHS recommends a dark, quiet room, using curtains, blinds, an eye mask or earplugs if you need them, and The Sleep Charity gives an ideal bedroom temperature of around 16-18°C. A comfortable mattress, pillows and covers matter too, and a tidy room with fresh bedding is more inviting at the end of the day.

Limit Screen Time Before Bed

The NHS advises against watching television or using devices such as smartphones right before bed, because the blue light makes you more awake. NHS Every Mind Matters suggests trying not to use phones, tablets or computers for an hour before bed. Reading a book, gentle stretching or calming music are easy swaps.

Be Mindful of Diet and Caffeine Intake

Caffeine, alcohol and heavy meals late in the day can all get in the way of sleep. The NHS advises not drinking alcohol, tea or coffee for at least 6 hours before bed, and not eating a big meal late at night. NHS Every Mind Matters suggests a warm milky drink or herbal tea as an evening alternative. Regular exercise during the day helps as well, though the NHS advises against exercising in the 4 hours before bed, and our guide on how to start a fitness journey has ideas for getting going.

Building a Bedtime Routine for Better Sleep

Developing a Calming Pre-Bed Ritual

A soothing bedtime routine helps your mind and body get ready for rest. The NHS suggests relaxing for at least an hour before bed, for example by taking a bath or reading a book. Some people also like gentle yoga, journalling or a guided meditation, and our round-up of the best meditation apps in the UK is a good place to start.

Recommended Bedtime Routine Ideas

Try winding down with a warm bath, a book or slow, deep breathing. The key is choosing things that relax you and doing them each night, so they become a signal that the day is over. Keep it simple enough that you will stick with it.

What to Avoid Before Bedtime

Bright lights, work tasks and difficult conversations can make it harder to switch off. Setting a time in the evening to put devices away, and using dimmer lighting in the last hour, can make the move to sleep smoother. If you are still awake after about 20 minutes in bed, NHS Every Mind Matters suggests getting up, doing something relaxing and going back to bed when you feel sleepy.

Sleep Hygiene and Mental Health

Sleep and mental health are closely linked: worry can keep you awake, and poor sleep can make worries feel bigger. A steady routine and a peaceful bedroom are sensible first steps, but they are not a treatment for anxiety or depression. If low mood or anxiety is affecting your sleep, speak to your GP, and see our guide to mental health support options in the UK.

Comparing Sleep Environments: What Works Best?

City vs Suburban vs Rural Bedrooms

The right fixes depend on where you live. In a city, street lighting and traffic noise are the usual problems, so blackout curtains or blinds and earplugs earn their keep. In quieter suburban and rural homes, early summer light and a room that is too warm or too cold are more likely to wake you. Every UK home can be made better for sleep with a few targeted changes.

Bedding and Mattress Choices

The NHS advice is simple: make sure your mattress, pillows and covers are comfortable. If you wake up stiff or your mattress has started to sag, it may be time to look at a new one, and The Sleep Charity suggests replacing pillows every two to three years. Breathable bedding that is easy to wash is a practical choice.

Addressing Allergies and Sensitivities

If you have allergies or sensitive skin, washing bedding regularly helps keep dust and other irritants down, and some people prefer fragrance-free laundry products. If you think an allergy is disturbing your sleep, a pharmacist or your GP can advise.

When to Seek Help: Persistent Sleep Problems

Signs It Is More Than a Bad Habit

Good sleep habits do not fix everything. The NHS describes insomnia as regularly finding it hard to go to sleep, waking up several times in the night, or waking early and not being able to get back to sleep. Loud snoring with breathing that stops and starts, or gasping noises in the night, can be signs of sleep apnoea, which the NHS says should be checked by a GP.

When to See Your GP

See your GP if sleep problems persist. The NHS says to see a GP if changing your sleeping habits has not helped, you have had trouble sleeping for months, or your insomnia is affecting your daily life in a way that makes it hard to cope. Your GP will try to find out what is causing it, and may sometimes offer cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). This guide does not cover sleeping tablets or supplements, so ask your GP or pharmacist before taking anything for sleep.

Sleep Hygiene Checklist

  • Go to bed and wake up at the same time daily.

  • Keep your bedroom cool, dark, and quiet.

  • Limit screens and caffeine in the evening.

  • Follow a calming bedtime routine.

  • Deal with noise, light or allergy problems in the bedroom.

  • See your GP if sleep problems persist.

Further Resources for UK Residents

The NHS website has practical advice on insomnia, and its Every Mind Matters pages cover how to fall asleep faster and sleep better. The Sleep Charity also publishes free guidance on sleep environments and routines. For the full official advice, read the NHS insomnia page.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the key tips for better sleep hygiene in the UK?

Stick to a regular sleep and wake time, keep your bedroom dark, quiet and cool, avoid screens, caffeine and alcohol before bed, and give yourself an hour to wind down. These are the habits the NHS suggests trying first.

How does screen time affect sleep quality at night?

The NHS advises against watching television or using devices such as smartphones right before bed, because the blue light makes you more awake. NHS Every Mind Matters suggests trying not to use phones, tablets or computers for an hour before bed.

What should I include in a sleep hygiene checklist?

A good checklist covers a consistent sleep schedule, a dark, quiet and cool bedroom, no caffeine, alcohol or screens late in the evening, and a relaxing wind-down routine. Add a reminder to see your GP if sleep problems persist.

Are there UK-specific guidelines for sleep hygiene?

Yes. The NHS publishes sleep advice on its insomnia pages and through Every Mind Matters, and The Sleep Charity offers guidance on bedrooms and routines. They are guidance to follow at home, not legal rules.

When should I seek professional help for sleep issues?

See your GP if sleep problems persist after you have changed your habits, if you have had trouble sleeping for months, or if it is affecting your daily life. Loud snoring with breathing that stops and starts should also be checked by a GP.
